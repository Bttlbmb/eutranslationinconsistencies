Research note / 15 October 2026

AI review of EU language versions: four experiments

Models, request layout, API processing and impact classification for Directive 2009/24/EC.

These experiments tested alternatives to the website’s usual procedure: two independent Sol scans followed by an Astra evidence review. The measurements covered supported issues, false positives, language coverage, cost and runtime.

Cached Sol scans and reviews reached the project’s $1–3 processing target in several runs. In the latest test, medium thinking cost $2.08 without impact assessment and $2.18 with it. Higher thinking recovered some additional supported issues, but cost more and took longer. Severity labels also needed a separate source check.

Common setup

Source material and scoring

All four experiments used the same preserved version of Directive 2009/24/EC on computer programs, dated 23 April 2009: the complete English text and 22 other language versions. English served as a common reference; it has no higher legal authority.

Baseline. The existing review used two Sol scans and the more capable Astra model at high thinking for evidence review. It produced 36 published findings. Its recorded analysis window was about 32 minutes, including a stop and resume; its API-equivalent cost was estimated at $6.32 including review follow-ups. This exceeds the project’s $1–3 target, and repeating that workload across the catalogue creates a substantial time cost. It is useful as a comparison using stronger models, but is not considered sustainable as the routine API configuration for this project.

There is no absolute answer key here. The first three source audits supported 33 or 34 of the baseline’s 36 findings, depending on the audit. Both the baseline and the alternatives remain model judgments that can miss issues or misread the law.

Luna was the lower-cost model; Sol performed scans and reviews; Astra audited the results. A “scan” looks for differences; a reviewer checks its suggestions. Independent scans did not see each other’s answers. Every suggestion with valid source quotations received review, including suggestions found only once. Zero-candidate results also received a coverage review.

The Astra audits received all available language versions and claims with their producing setup hidden. The tables count distinct language-specific issues accepted by the trial reviewer, then classified by Astra: supported means the audit found a plausible, substantial change in legal meaning; false positive means it did not; uncertain means the evidence did not settle it. Coverage passed counts languages clearing the reviewer’s completeness check; it does not prove every issue was found. These are AI judgments, not human legal adjudication.

Experiment 01

Models and number of scans

The first test compared one, two or three Luna scans with one or two Sol scans. Discovery used medium thinking; Sol review used high thinking. Higher-count setups reused the earlier independent scans. These runs used the Codex subscription; dollar figures estimate API-equivalent cost from recorded usage.

The runtime column estimates running each setup on its own with two concurrent model calls, using saved call durations. It excludes the final Astra audit. Because the experiment shared scans and interleaved setups, these are scheduling estimates, not measured standalone elapsed times.

Recorded experiment time: 154 minutes for the model variants, 14 minutes for the Astra audit; 168 minutes from first model call to final audit result.

Interpretation. Three Luna scans produced 28 supported issues, compared with 43 from one Sol scan. The two-Sol setup had four more supported issues at about 48% more estimated cost. A third Luna scan left the supported total at 28. Five false positives in the three-Luna setup had been found by at least two scans, so agreement alone would have accepted repeated errors.

Experiment 02

Request layout and reviewer thinking in Codex

Each setup used one Sol scan and one Sol reviewer. Requests contained one, two or four target languages with their complete texts. Other variants reduced reviewer thinking or requested shorter answers. Source text was not shortened. A coverage objection withheld confirmation for every language in that group.

Recorded experiment time: 95 minutes for the model variants, 11 minutes for the Astra audit; 106 minutes from first model call to final audit result.

Interpretation. Grouping four languages reduced estimated cost to $1.53, with ten languages confirmed under the group coverage rule. Medium reviewer thinking retained a supported total of 37 and cost about 11% less than high thinking. The shorter-answer variant produced more false positives and a higher estimated cost. The individual-language layout with medium review was selected for the API experiment.

Experiment 03

API processing and caching

Paid API calls ran with a $25 cap. Each setup used a Sol scan and separate Sol review at medium thinking. Explicit caching put shared English text before variable target texts and candidates. Flex used lower-priority requests; Batch queued discovery and review as two successive jobs. Both applied a 50% processing discount at the recorded rates.

The fifth setup reused cached standard processing and added independent high-thinking Sol review in four languages flagged for uncertainty or coverage gaps. High reviewers saw the source texts and candidates, but no earlier verdicts. Other coverage gaps received at most one fresh full-language scan and review. Unlike the first two tables, these results and prices include those follow-ups.

Recorded experiment time: 63 minutes for the model variants, 18 minutes for the Astra audit; 81 minutes from first model call to final audit result.

Interpretation. Selective extra review recovered all 33 reference findings supported by Astra in this audit, plus ten further supported issues. Flex produced the most supported issues, but its follow-ups raised false positives from seven to twelve without increasing the supported total. Standard automatic caching still left Lithuanian coverage unconfirmed.

Batch’s initial cycle took about an hour; Flex’s took about seven minutes. These overlapping runs are not controlled latency measurements. The five setups together used $6.16 of API processing across 204 calls, with reused calls charged only once. Batch follow-ups used standard processing; Flex follow-ups retained Flex pricing.

API runtimes add each setup’s observed initial and follow-up work periods, including Batch queueing. They exclude waits between phases while other setups ran. Up to four languages ran concurrently, and follow-ups shared a worker pool. These timings are therefore not directly comparable with the two-worker Codex estimates. The separate Astra audit is excluded from each row.

Experiment 04

Thinking level and impact classification

Six standard-API configurations tested medium or high thinking in the Sol scan or reviewer. Three also proposed severe or non-severe conditional impact. High thinking changed reasoning.effort; processing stayed standard throughout. Fast and Ultrafast were not used.

The runs kept the full source texts, explicit shared-English caching and four language workers. Medium-scan/high-review setups reused the corresponding initial medium scans, giving the reviewer identical candidates. Impact-on scans were independent of impact-off scans. Reviewers received no scan impact labels or earlier review verdicts.

Every source-valid candidate received review. Uncertainty, coverage gaps and unresolved or disputed impact triggered one independent high-thinking review. Any remaining coverage gap received at most one fresh full-language scan and review. The final results include this extra work.

Recorded experiment time: 139 minutes for the API variants, 172 minutes for the common Astra audit; 312 minutes from the first API call to the final audit result.

The audit window includes a prolonged network and model-service interruption. Two validated batches were retained; unfinished work resumed with four workers, increased from two. The same claims, prompts, sources, model and thinking settings were preserved. This interruption affected the benchmark audit, after all API variants had finished.

Observed time records the work actually executed for each setup. The two reused-scan rows omit their earlier scan time; the standalone estimate schedules all their required successful calls across four workers. These figures exclude the separate Astra audit and are not isolated latency benchmarks. The high-scan/no-impact configuration still had Lithuanian coverage unconfirmed.

The common Astra audit supported 34 of the 36 published baseline findings. It also rechecked the earlier recommended API output: 42 supported issues, 7 false positives and 3 uncertain issues. These are new judgments of saved output, not additional discovery. Comparisons within this audit are more consistent than comparisons with the earlier audits.

Initial cycle before follow-ups

These rows isolate the first scan and review. Within each impact condition, the medium/medium and medium/high rows share their scan candidates. High-scan comparisons use independent candidate sets, so they also include sampling variation.

What the impact labels mean

Severe means the text supports a concrete major consequence for an important entitlement, permission, duty, eligibility, remedy, financial liability or sanction if the disputed reading prevails. Non-severe means an identified narrower or ancillary consequence. Unresolved impact remains unassigned; it is not treated as non-severe. These are conditional AI judgments, not findings of actual harm.

Both stages supplied a label, scenario, rationale, uncertainty and exact supporting quotations. The test therefore measures that full assessment task, rather than the price of adding a bare label. Astra assessed the difference, assigned its own impact category and checked each proposed explanation separately.

Astra’s severity counts across the supported results

These counts apply Astra’s common impact assessment retrospectively to each setup’s supported distinct issues. They exclude rejected and uncertain differences. The earlier experiments did not originally use this binary scheme; only the earlier recommended API output was rechecked here. Rows overlap and cannot be added as a total of unique issues.

Impact proposals count individual claims; finding totals above combine repeated claims about the same issue in one language. Label agreement counts only supported differences where both models assigned a category. The last column requires both a supported difference and a supported explanation, out of all accepted claims; it does not require severity agreement. Astra saw no proposed category fields or producer identities, although the presence and wording of an impact explanation were visible.

On the identical initial impact candidates, medium and high reviewers accepted 43 claims in common (45 and 48 accepted in total). They agreed on 40 of 41 mutually assigned severity labels, while their finding verdicts differed on 10 of 65 candidates. An initial Maltese back-up-copy disagreement changed from severe to non-severe under high thinking, then returned to severe after an independent extra check. This measures consistency between Sol outputs, not correctness.

Cost, caching and the timed-out request

The six configurations used $16.00 of new metered API processing. Together with experiment 3, that is $22.16, plus at most $0.38 unresolved, within the shared $25 cap. Reused calls count once in that total and in full for each standalone setup. All thinking settings used the same token rates.

One Swedish high-thinking review reached the 15-minute read timeout without returning usage. Its possible charge remains reserved; an identical-input replacement returned in 76 seconds. The observed runtime includes the timeout and recovery. The successful-call scheduling estimate excludes the failed wait. Both attempts are retained.

Higher thinking used more billed internal reasoning: $0.53 in the medium/medium control, compared with $1.70 in the high-scan run. Repricing the control’s recorded usage without caching gives $2.55, versus its $2.08 metered cost: about 19% less with caching at identical token usage. Caching discounts repeated input, not newly generated reasoning or answers. Different candidates and follow-ups also affect these run totals.

For medium/medium thinking, adding the complete impact task increased the initial-cycle cost from $1.37 to $1.69, about $0.31. The final gap was only $0.10 because the impact run needed fewer coverage follow-ups. These independent runs do not establish a fixed per-label price.

High thinking found more supported issues in this test, with higher processing cost and runtime. High-scan configurations produced 46 supported issues, compared with 43 without impact and 40 with impact under medium thinking. The high scan without impact also had more false positives and left one language’s coverage unresolved. Raising reviewer thinking increased supported findings too, but did not consistently reduce false positives. These are single runs; the high-scan and impact-on conditions changed the candidate set as well as the instructions.

The severity results are encouraging but limited. Sol and Astra agreed on 33/35, 40/40 and 38/39 supported claims where both assigned a label. Calling every case severe would already agree on about 86–90% of those same claims. Sol correctly identified 3/5, 4/4 and 4/5 of Astra’s non-severe cases, respectively. These small, overlapping sets cannot establish general classification accuracy. Astra also rejected 3–4 claims labelled severe in each setup as differences at all.

The disagreements concern the scope of the claimed consequence. For the Maltese back-up-copy case, both final medium-scan outputs called the impact severe; Astra considered the supported effect ancillary because other software rights remained restricted. For several pre-1993 savings clauses, Astra accepted a possible textual difference but left impact unassigned: losing an express safeguard did not establish retrospective liability. More thinking did not consistently supply that missing legal context.

Integrated impact assessment can fit the $1–3 target at medium thinking: $2.18 here, versus $2.08 without it. That small final gap was partly due to fewer follow-ups; the initial impact task added about $0.31. The more expensive impact setups cost $3.67 and $3.86. A larger pilot should test whether their additional supported findings justify that cost, while continuing to check the underlying difference and the proposed consequence separately.

Extrapolation

Cost per 1,000 words and a longer-act example

The test directive contains 2,894 English words in the preserved reader text. For the experiment 3 table, dividing each API cost by 2.894 gives a provisional rate per 1,000 English words for comparison with 22 target languages. This covers a fresh Sol scan and Sol review, including the observed follow-ups. It excludes impact assessment and the separate Astra audit.

For the explicitly cached setups in experiment 3, that rate is roughly $0.30–0.60 per 1,000 English words across 22 target languages. It is a normalization of one short act, rather than a validated rule for longer legislation. Fixed request overhead, candidate numbers, reasoning and repeat reviews need not grow in proportion to word count.

A conditional calculation is: cost ≈ observed rate × (English words ÷ 1,000) × (target languages ÷ 22). English is only the length reference; translation lengths and token counts differ by language. The rates below use each setup’s unrounded cost.

The preserved Digital Services Act, Regulation (EU) 2022/2065, contains 63,846 English words in its original version dated 19 October 2022. That is 22.1 times the directive’s length. Its 23 target languages, including Irish, make the combined scaling factor 23.1. These projections describe a fresh review of all those languages; the website’s existing DSA review remains partial.

Budget implication. Using experiment 3’s rates under these assumptions, reviewing the DSA would cost about $21–38 for one cached Sol + Sol configuration. Selective high review gives about $38; Flex gives about $22. A $1–3 budget therefore fits an act of a few thousand words much more readily than a text of this length. These are conditional processing estimates, not spending limits or a guarantee of comparable quality.

Experiment 4 shows the variation between runs: the medium-thinking repeat cost $2.08 without impact assessment and $2.18 with it, or $0.72 and $0.75 per 1,000 English words. The same arithmetic would put a DSA review near $48 or $50, with roughly 4.9 or 5.7 active hours. These are further conditional examples, not new DSA measurements; the earlier $21–38 range is not a firm budget. Neither estimate includes the separate benchmark audit.

Time implication. For experiment 3, simply multiplying the observed active times gives about 3.4–4.0 hours for the non-Batch options. This is arithmetic, not a reliable completion-time forecast. The original runs processed up to four languages concurrently; long texts, splitting, rate limits and service availability change that relationship. Batch queueing cannot be extrapolated from word count. Flex can be slower or temporarily unavailable, even though it was quick in this trial.

Long acts may require aligned sections, repeated context and extra checks of definitions and cross-references. Those add tokens and work. Sol pricing also increases above 272,000 input tokens per request: input and cache rates double and output rates rise by 50% for the whole request. The projection assumes that surcharge is avoided. Count tokens for each language pair and test a representative set of sections before setting a firm DSA budget or deadline. This calculation does not establish that the short-act workflow retains its coverage or accuracy on the DSA.

Assessment

Limitations and next tests

This is one short directive, with one fresh set of outputs for most setups. Each experiment had its own Astra audit. Those judgments vary, so comparisons within each table are more useful than a league table across all four.

The existing reports were rechecked as hypotheses. Passing coverage did not eliminate false positives or translation errors. Follow-ups replaced the corresponding trial results while preserving earlier attempts; they could lose previously supported issues. The experiment did not measure every issue that the models might have missed.

Cached Sol scans and reviews with medium thinking and selective extra checks remain a reasonable starting point for a larger pilot. The latest repeat cost $2.08, or $2.18 with integrated impact assessment. High thinking improved some results, but did not consistently remove false positives or resolve severity. Further testing should cover several acts, repeated runs, longer texts and a separate evaluation set. The published review procedure has not changed.

Cost accounting and model settings

The first two tables show initial cycles, without coverage follow-ups. Their prices are API-equivalent estimates from available Codex usage logs, with caching disabled for comparison; some failed attempts had no usage record. They are not amounts paid. The third and fourth experiments use returned API usage at the rates recorded on 15 October 2026, including cache reads, writes and reasoning output; these totals have not been reconciled to an invoice.

Prices cover discovery and Sol evidence review. The first three experiments excluded impact assessment; experiment 4 includes it where indicated. All tables exclude the separate Astra benchmark audit. The audits used the existing Codex subscription, adding no API charge. Some runs were reused across setups; each table price represents running that setup on its own.

Charges depend on tokens, small pieces of text, in both requests and responses. The cached standard initial run in experiment 3 had 248,850 cache-read tokens out of 540,308 input tokens. Repricing that same usage without caching gives $1.80, compared with its $1.33 recorded cost: about 26% less. This is a price calculation, not a second run.

Length counts use the same method as the catalogue: whitespace-separated words containing a letter or number, across all preserved English passages, including introductory and closing units. The longer-act calculation scales the complete observed price, including output, reasoning and follow-ups. It assumes similar candidate density, repeat work, cache reuse and token volume per English word, with unchanged rates and no added segmentation overhead. One act cannot establish those assumptions, separate fixed from per-word costs, or provide a prediction interval. The DSA figures concern the archived original act, not a later consolidation.

Codex runtime estimates schedule discovery calls across two worker slots, then reviewer calls across two slots; they include the logged call durations but exclude startup and judging. API times sum observed active phases, rather than claiming an isolated standalone benchmark. Experiment totals are recorded first-call-to-audit windows, including orchestration gaps. The baseline’s 32-minute figure comes from its two analysis-job records and excludes importing and publication. Its review used six language groups, so workflow and concurrency also differ from the later trials.

Models: gpt-6-luna, gpt-6.1-sol, and gpt-6-astra. Discovery used medium thinking in experiments 1–3; experiment 4 also tested high thinking. Sol review used high thinking in experiment 1; experiment 2 varied it; experiment 3 used medium thinking with the stated selective high-thinking checks. Astra audits used high thinking. Changes in message layout, new generations and scheduling mean this was not a perfectly isolated test of caching alone.
